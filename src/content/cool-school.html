Cool School · Game redesign

A school game made for today’s kids

Reimagining a conflict-resolution game for ages 4–7 as a playful school world kids can explore, shape, and return to.

Audience Children ages 4–7
Team Four-person design research team
My role Research · Co-design · Environment design
Focus Game redesign · Co-design · Research

The starting point

A meaningful lesson in an outdated game

Cool School was created to teach young children how to handle conflicts at school. Players resolved 26 conflicts, each linked to a letter-based trophy; collecting A through Z meant the game was complete.

The game had become dated. Our four-person design research team set out to make it engaging for today’s children and relevant for longer, while keeping its extensively researched learning mechanism.

The design shift

Keep the learning. Give kids more reasons to play.

We expanded the experience around conflict resolution: an open school to explore, characters and places to discover, and playful activities that make the world feel alive between learning moments.

Inside the redesign

A world to explore. A character to make your own.

01 · Explore & play

Make the whole school part of the game.

Children can move between and inside school buildings, meet characters, and discover spontaneous interactions—some tied to conflicts, some just for fun. Mini-games like playing fetch add more to do.

A school with room to roam

Explore between buildings.
Meet someone new.
Stop and play.

02 · Make it yours

A character that feels like their own.

Customizable avatars let children express themselves. Accessories earned with XP and coins extend that sense of ownership as they play.

03 · Keep progressing

Give every visit a new goal.

Daily achievements and an open-ended number of conflicts replace the original fixed A-to-Z finish line. Players earn XP and coins, then spend coins on avatar accessories.

Reflection

This redesign explored how the game could feel more open-ended and inviting without losing sight of what it was built to teach. The work combined children's perspectives, expert feedback, and familiar game patterns with a refreshed visual direction.
